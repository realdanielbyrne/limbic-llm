# Dentate gyrus to CA3: pattern separation and mixture-of-experts routing in limbic-llm

*Review of the DG → CA3 track of [limbic-llm](https://github.com/realdanielbyrne/limbic-llm) at commit 3799b36. Scope: `limbic_llm/dentate_gyrus.py`, the `"dg"` addressing mode of `limbic_llm/gated_delta.py`, the `--dg-keys` path of `temporal_loop.py`, register entries M2, M7, T13, T16 and backlog W4, and the "DG → MoE gate" framing in `docs/genesis.md` and `REFERENCES.md`.*

## Summary

The repository's dentate gyrus is a learned linear expansion followed by k-winners-take-all (k-WTA) and a per-unit gain. In machine-learning terms this is a top-k sparse feed-forward layer, and when its output is used as a write key for a delta-rule memory it is a sparse-address associative memory in the sense of Kanerva. Both constructions are well published. The framing of the DG as a mixture-of-experts (MoE) *gate* is only partly sound. It holds formally in the fine-grained limit, where every granule cell is a width-one expert, as in product-key memories and PEER. It fails functionally for the coarse routers in Qwen3, Qwen3-Next and Qwen3.5. Those routers are trained to send similar tokens to the same experts under a load-balancing constraint; that is clustering, closer to pattern completion than to separation. The repository's own evidence for a DG benefit is thin and single-seed. M7 is a ceiling effect, T13 is one seed with two arms, and T16 contradicts the classical prediction for a reason the design does not control. The most valuable next steps are an overlap-controlled capacity test of sparse keys above the key dimension, a measurement of whether Qwen MoE router codes separate or cluster near-duplicate inputs, and an expert-neurogenesis continual-learning test on a Qwen MoE. The last connects directly to published expert-restricted and memory-slot-restricted fine-tuning.

## Scope

This review covers three questions. The first is what the biology and theory of the DG actually claim: expansion recoding, sparse coding, pattern separation versus completion, mossy-fibre "detonator" transmission, and adult neurogenesis. The second is which machine-learning work already implements each element: k-WTA activations, random expansion hashing, sparse distributed memory, product-key and memory layers, MoE routing and its pathologies, MoE-based continual learning, and sparse linear-attention state updates. The third is whether "DG as MoE gate" is a defensible analogy, and what a faithful DG → CA3 module would look like on a pretrained Qwen MoE. The CA3 associative store, the CA1 comparator and the CA2 side channel are treated only where they bear on DG addressing. Model-configuration figures for Qwen3.5 come from secondary web sources because the model cards on Hugging Face were not reachable from the review environment; they are flagged as provisional where sources disagree.

## Theory and evidence for the dentate gyrus

### Expansion, sparsity and separation

The computational case for the DG rests on the claim that projecting inputs into a much larger population under strong competition decorrelates them before storage. [Marr 1971](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1971.0078) introduced codon-style expansion recoding for archicortex. [McNaughton & Morris 1987](https://doi.org/10.1016/0166-2236%2887%2990011-7) argued that the sparse, powerful mossy-fibre input could act as a teaching signal that forces new CA3 patterns. [Treves & Rolls 1992](https://doi.org/10.1002/hipo.450020209) and [Treves & Rolls 1994](https://doi.org/10.1002/hipo.450040319) made the division of labour quantitative: the DG supplies sparse, decorrelated patterns for *storage*, while retrieval cues arrive by the direct perforant path and are completed by CA3 recurrents. [O'Reilly & McClelland 1994](https://doi.org/10.1002/hipo.450040605) showed that separation and completion trade off within a single network and that the DG–CA3 arrangement escapes the trade-off. The theory of expansion layers has since been generalised beyond the hippocampus. [Babadi & Sompolinsky 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.07.035) showed that random expansion with sparsification improves downstream classification only when the coding level is tuned, and that noise in the input limits the benefit. [Litwin-Kumar et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2017.01.030) derived an optimal in-degree for expansion layers that matches the cerebellum and mushroom body. [Cayco-Gajic & Silver 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.01.044) argue that expansion, sparse connectivity, thresholding and inhibition each contribute separately and should not be collapsed into a single "pattern separation" label. That warning applies directly to a k-WTA module whose expansion weights are trained end to end.

### Physiological evidence and its complications

Physiological evidence supports DG separation, but it is less clean than the textbook account. [Leutgeb et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1135801) found that small changes in environment shape changed DG firing strongly while CA3 changed only at larger morphs. [Bakker et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152882) reported a separation-like signal in human CA3/DG with high-resolution fMRI, and [Yassa & Stark 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tins.2011.06.006) consolidated these results into the input-overlap versus output-overlap "transfer function" view. [Neunuebel & Knierim 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.11.017) supplied the most direct double dissociation: under local–global cue conflict, DG representations decorrelated while CA3 stayed coherent, which is evidence for CA3 completion. Cell identity complicates the picture. [Neunuebel & Knierim 2012](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.6038-11.2012), [GoodSmith et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.12.026) and [Senzai & Buzsáki 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.12.011) showed that many active, multi-field "DG" units in earlier recordings were probably hilar mossy cells, while granule cells are mostly silent or single-field. Which population carries the separation signal is therefore not settled. [Chawla et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1002/hipo.20091) put the active granule-cell fraction at a few percent, which supports the repository's 2–4 % figure. A recent preprint, [Hu et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.64898/2025.12.04.692471), reports that DG input sharpens rate coding in proximal CA3 without driving global remapping. If it holds, DG separation acts on firing rates within a stable code rather than by assigning a new code. Reviews by [Rolls 2013](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2013.00074) and [Knierim & Neunuebel 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nlm.2015.10.008) summarise the attractor-based account that the repository relies on.

### Mossy fibres and inhibition

Mossy-fibre transmission is conditional, not a fixed gain. [Henze et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn887) found that single granule cells can discharge CA3 targets in vivo, mainly when they fire in bursts. [Vyleta et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.7554/elife.17977) showed that full "detonation" depends on short-term facilitation and post-tetanic potentiation. The functional consequence is that the DG → CA3 drive is gated by recent granule-cell activity, which suits a teacher signal that acts at encoding. A static learned gain per unit, as in `DentateGyrus.log_gain`, does not capture this. The hilus is also more than an inhibitory loop. [Scharfman 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn.2016.87) reviews evidence that mossy cells are glutamatergic and excite granule cells directly while also recruiting inhibition. They are not inhibitory interneurons.

### Adult neurogenesis

Computational accounts of adult neurogenesis disagree about whether new neurons increase or decrease separation. [Becker 2005](https://doi.org/10.1002/hipo.20095) proposed that neuronal turnover maintains separation in a sparse code under continued learning. [Aimone et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2008.11.026) and [Aimone et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2011.05.010) argued that immature, hyperexcitable, broadly tuned granule cells perform *integration*: they link events close in time, while mature cells separate. [Finnegan & Becker 2015](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2015.00136) found that neurogenesis can lower measured separation while still reducing interference. The repository's young-neuron learning-rate boost has physiological support in the enhanced plasticity of 4–6-week-old cells ([Schmidt-Hieber et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature02553); [Ge et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2007.05.002)). In deep networks, [Tran et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2206704119) showed that neurogenesis-like reinitialisation of units acts as a regulariser, and [Draelos et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1109/ijcnn.2017.7965898) added units to autoencoder layers to accommodate new classes. The human data are contested: [Spalding et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2013.05.002) reported substantial adult turnover, [Sorrells et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature25975) found it undetectable in adults, and [Moreno-Jiménez et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-019-0375-9) found it abundant with different tissue handling. The repository cites only Spalding.

## Closest prior work in machine learning

### Sparse expansion codes and SDM

Every component of the repository's `DentateGyrus` exists in published sparse-coding work, so this module should be treated as a reimplementation. [Makhzani & Frey 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.5663) introduced k-sparse autoencoders, and [Ahmad & Scheinkman 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.11257) (preprint) used k-WTA layers with boosting to obtain noise-robust sparse representations. [Dasgupta et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aam9868) showed that the fly olfactory circuit, a random sparse expansion followed by winner-take-all, is a locality-sensitive hash. Note the direction: FlyHash *preserves* similarity neighbourhoods in a sparse code. It is not maximally decorrelating, and this is the distinction the DG literature cares about. [Shen et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco_a_01615) used the same circuit with partial-freezing associative learning to reduce catastrophic forgetting. Kanerva's Sparse Distributed Memory (Kanerva 1988, MIT Press, ISBN 0-262-11132-2) stores data at many sparsely activated hard locations, and it already includes the heteroassociative sequence mode in which the current pattern is the address for the next. [Bricken & Pehlevan 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.05498) showed that softmax attention approximates SDM read-out under realistic conditions. [Bricken et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11934) turned SDM into an MLP with top-k activation, L2-normalised weights and no bias, and found that it learns continually without replay; every one of those components was needed. That paper is the closest published analogue to `DentateGyrus` used as a continual learner, and the repository does not cite it. [Iyer et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbot.2022.846219) combined k-WTA with context-gated dendritic segments for multi-task learning. [Sarfraz et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05058) and [Alonso & Krichmar 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-46976-4) used sparse coding inside dual-memory and Hopfield-style continual learners. [Gershman et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2025.02.029) set out the key–value view of hippocampal memory, in which separated keys and content-rich values are distinct representations. That is the conceptual frame for the repository's choice of DG codes as keys and the stream as values.

### Fine-grained experts and memory layers

The literature that makes the DG-as-MoE analogy exact is the fine-grained limit of MoE, not the coarse Switch/GShard-style router. [Zhang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01786) showed that a dense FFN can be split into experts post hoc because its activations are already sparse, and [Li et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06313) found that trained transformer FFNs show emergent activation sparsity and work with explicit top-k. [Lample et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.05242) introduced product-key memory, a top-k lookup over about a million learned keys with value vectors. [He 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04153) (preprint) extended it to PEER, over a million single-neuron experts selected by product keys. [Berges et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09764) (preprint) scaled memory layers to billions of slots. A PEER layer *is* expansion plus k-WTA, with each selected unit contributing its own output vector. It is therefore a closer computational match to granule cells with their mossy-fibre outputs than the repository's own module, which emits the sparse code itself and leaves the output mapping to the next layer. [Roller et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.04426) showed that fixed hash routing is competitive with learned routing, which is the MoE analogue of a random, unlearned DG projection.

### Routing, load balancing and collapse

Coarse MoE routers differ from the DG in their objective, and the difference is the crux of this review. [Shazeer et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.06538) and [Fedus et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961) train token-choice top-k gates with auxiliary load-balancing losses. [Zhou et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09368) invert the choice so that each expert picks its top tokens. [Dai et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06066) introduced fine-grained segmentation plus always-on shared experts. [Wang et al. 2024a](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15664) (preprint) replaced the auxiliary loss with per-expert bias adjustment. [Qiu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11873) showed that computing the balance loss over the global batch rather than the micro-batch produces more domain-specialised experts. [Chi et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09179) found that routing tends to collapse token representations toward expert centroids. The neuroscience mapping is direct. Token-choice top-k is *population* sparseness: few units active per input. Load balancing and expert choice impose *lifetime* sparseness: each unit active for few inputs. A router that sends near-duplicate tokens to the same experts, and that collapses representations toward centroids, performs clustering. Clustering is completion-like. The DG is defined by the opposite input–output overlap relation.

### Qwen MoE router designs

The Qwen models on which the repository runs illustrate how coarse current routers are relative to a DG. The [Qwen3 report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388) states that its MoE models have 128 experts with 8 active per token, exclude shared experts, and use the global-batch balancing loss of Qiu et al. The [Qwen3-Next blog](https://qwen.ai/blog?id=4074cca80393150c248e508aa62983f9cb7d27cd&from=research.latest-advancements-list) describes 512 experts with 10 routed plus 1 shared, global load balancing, and router normalisation at initialisation. It interleaves Gated DeltaNet with gated attention at 3:1. For Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, secondary sources disagree. A [vLLM recipe](https://recipes.vllm.ai/Qwen/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B) gives 256 experts, and an [independent implementation note](https://swedishembedded.com/docs/brain/reference/models/qwen35moe) describes 256 experts with top-8 and a gated shared expert. [NVIDIA's bridge documentation](https://docs.nvidia.com/nemo/megatron-bridge/latest/models/vlm/qwen35-vl.html) lists "512 experts with top-10 routing" under the Qwen3.5 MoE family, apparently for the 397B model. Treat 256/8+1 for the 35B model as provisional until it is read from `config.json`. In every case the routed code is about 2–6 % active over at most 512 units per layer. The DG has about 10^6 granule cells. Concatenated across 40–48 layers, however, the set of selected experts is a binary code of about 10^4 dimensions with a few hundred active bits. Its separation properties have, as far as this review found, never been measured.

### MoE and sparsity for continual learning

Expert addition for continual learning is established, so "neurogenesis = new experts" is a re-description. [Aljundi et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.06194) gated a growing set of task experts with autoencoder reconstruction error. [Chen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12281) (preprint) added experts during lifelong pretraining with regularisation. [Yu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.11549) used MoE adapters on a frozen CLIP. [Wang et al. 2024b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18886) (preprint) expanded adapters on demand when a representation-shift detector fired. [Rypeść et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10191) trained only one selected expert per task. [Kapoor et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.16651) (preprint, already in the repository's bibliography) is the closest bio-inspired precedent. HiCL routes inputs to experts by cosine similarity between top-k sparse DG codes and per-task DG prototypes, with a CA3-like store, replay and EWC. It anticipates the "DG-gated MoE" idea at small scale. Two LLM-scale results bear directly on the author's separate project of restricting fine-tuning to functional subnetworks. [Wang et al. 2024c](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01906) found that task routing in a fine-grained MoE is concentrated, and that tuning only the most-routed experts matches full fine-tuning. [Lin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15103) (preprint) updated only memory-layer slots that new data activates much more than pretraining data does. NaturalQuestions F1 fell by 11 % after learning new facts, against 89 % for full fine-tuning and 71 % for LoRA. Both are subnetwork-restricted fine-tuning, with the subnetwork defined by a sparse code. In DG terms, the sparse code defines which units a new memory owns.

### Sparse addressing in linear-attention memories

The `"dg"` addressing mode of `GatedDeltaNet` has close recent analogues in the linear-attention literature. [Schlag et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11174) already expanded keys with a deterministic parameter-free projection to raise the capacity of fast-weight memories, and identified capacity as bounded by key dimension. [Pan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16577) (preprint) made the state update row-sparse by softmax top-k "classification" of each token into state partitions, explicitly to reduce inter-class interference, and scaled it to a 2B hybrid. That is essentially k-WTA addressing of a delta-style state. [Du et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13685) (preprint) route each token to one of several independent linear-memory states with a learned router to reduce interference. That is an MoE over CA3-like memories. [Zhang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07146) (preprint) use a softmax over a bounded set of memory slots. The repository's successor binding, where the address of token t−1 keys the write of token t, is Kanerva's sequence-storage scheme implemented with the delta rule of [Yang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06464). The repository's description of it as heteroassociative CA3 sequence memory echoes [Samsonovich & Ascoli 2005](https://doi.org/10.1101/lm.85205), whose CA3 stores successor links between place codes and retrieves paths by following them.

## Novelty assessment

The DG module itself is not novel. Expansion plus k-WTA plus an age-dependent plasticity boost for reserve units reimplements k-sparse layers ([Makhzani & Frey 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.5663); [Ahmad & Scheinkman 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.11257)), the SDM-MLP of [Bricken et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11934), and neurogenesis-style unit addition ([Draelos et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1109/ijcnn.2017.7965898); [Tran et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2206704119)). The "ICA-flavoured" decorrelation loss is a generic off-diagonal covariance penalty. None of this is a problem for a research codebase, but the README and genesis should present it as a reimplementation.

The "DG as MoE gate" framing is anticipated where it is sound and unsound where it is new. As a DG-gated MoE for continual learning it is anticipated by HiCL. As a router-free sparse selection of tiny experts it is anticipated by PKM and PEER. As a description of what a Qwen-style coarse router does, it is functionally mistaken, because those routers cluster. The repository's own lab notebook (R1) already says that nothing reuses an MoE router and that routers are trained for balance, not separation. That correction has not propagated to `REFERENCES.md`, which still annotates Shazeer et al. as "DG as an expert gate", or to the genesis narrative.

The sparse write addresses in a delta-rule memory are anticipated in substance by Kanerva's SDM, by the key expansion of Schlag et al., and most specifically by Sparse State Expansion (Pan et al. 2025) and Mixture-of-Memories (Du et al. 2025). What remains specific to limbic-llm is the combination with successor binding in a side-channel memory attached to a *frozen* hybrid model. A second specific element is the observation (T13) that sparse write keys counteract self-rehearsal primacy in a closed re-injection loop. The first is an engineering combination; the second is a new but single-seed observation.

Two directions appear open in the literature searched. One is using a pretrained MoE's router selections, concatenated across layers, as the sparse key code of a separate associative memory, while also measuring whether those codes separate or cluster. The other is an explicit test of the delta-rule capacity bound with sparse versus dense keys under controlled key overlap. Both are proposed below. Neither has been run, so they are opportunities rather than contributions.

## Biological accuracy in the mapping

The `DentateGyrus` docstring attributes feedback inhibition to "basket / mossy cells". Mossy cells are excitatory glutamatergic neurons that both excite granule cells and drive interneurons ([Scharfman 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn.2016.87)). The text should say basket cells and other GABAergic interneurons, and could add mossy-cell excitatory feedback as a separate pathway. That pathway matters because mossy cells, not granule cells, may carry much of the recorded remapping ([GoodSmith et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.12.026); [Senzai & Buzsáki 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.12.011)).

The "mossy-fibre gain" is a static per-unit scale applied to the code. Biologically, mossy-fibre efficacy is frequency-dependent and conditional ([Henze et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn887); [Vyleta et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.7554/elife.17977)), and its proposed role is to impose patterns on CA3 at encoding ([McNaughton & Morris 1987](https://doi.org/10.1016/0166-2236%2887%2990011-7)). The faithful analogue in the repository's own terms is a write-strength term β_t that grows when the same DG units fire repeatedly, applied to writes and not reads. A learned constant gain is not that.

Newborn units in `grow()` are initialised at Kaiming scale and then compete under the same global k-WTA as mature units with trained weights. Biologically, immature granule cells are hyperexcitable and broadly tuned, and they are preferentially recruited ([Aimone et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2011.05.010)). Whether newborn units in the repository ever win the top-k, or instead win all the time, depends on the scale of the trained weights and is not monitored. This is a code-review observation; it was not run here. A per-unit excitability bias that decays with age would be closer to biology and would make recruitment controllable.

The repository frames DG separation as decorrelation of arbitrary inputs. The theoretical literature says a random sparse expansion preserves similarity neighbourhoods ([Dasgupta et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aam9868)) and decorrelates moderately overlapping inputs only at an appropriate coding level ([Babadi & Sompolinsky 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.07.035)). With trained expansion weights, backpropagation can just as easily *align* codes for inputs that share a target, the opposite of separation. The repository's measure of DG separation is a single cosine comparison in a unit test, `pattern_separation`. It needs to be replaced by an input-overlap versus output-overlap curve measured on the trained model ([Yassa & Stark 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tins.2011.06.006)).

T16 reports that a direct dense read completes noisy cues worse than a DG read, contrary to Treves–Rolls. The Treves–Rolls argument relies on CA3 recurrent collaterals to complete a partial direct-path cue ([Treves & Rolls 1992](https://doi.org/10.1002/hipo.450020209); [Neunuebel & Knierim 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.11.017)). The temporal loop's read is a single linear lookup with no recurrent completion stage. The k-WTA on the DG read acts as a denoising threshold that the direct path lacks. T16 therefore tests "thresholded read versus unthresholded read", not the Treves–Rolls hypothesis. It should be relabelled until a completion stage is present.

Finally, the human adult-neurogenesis premise should carry its controversy ([Sorrells et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature25975); [Moreno-Jiménez et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-019-0375-9)). The computational argument for reserve units does not depend on it, but the citation to [Spalding et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2013.05.002) alone overstates consensus.

## Proposed experiments

### Overlap-controlled capacity of sparse keys

The hypothesis is that sparse keys cannot raise the *exact* capacity of a linear delta-rule memory above its key dimension d_k. Exact recall of N arbitrary values requires N linearly independent keys, whatever their sparsity. Above d_k, and for highly overlapping inputs, sparse keys should degrade more gracefully because each write touches only k columns of the state, as Pan et al. argue for row-sparse updates. The design reuses `experiments/gdn_capacity.py`. Keys are generated with controlled pairwise input cosine ρ ∈ {0, 0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95} through shared-prefix or noise mixing. Load runs over N/d_k ∈ {0.5, 1, 2, 4}. The arms are `gdn_x8`, `tied_x8` and `dg_x8`, with k-WTA sparsity in {1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 100} %, and a fixed random FlyHash projection at matched sparsity to separate "sparse" from "learned". Each arm runs at least three seeds. The metrics are recall accuracy versus load and ρ, retention of the oldest pairs, and the measured input–output overlap curve of the trained addresses. This resolves M7, which was confounded by a ceiling effect, and the condition named in RESULTS §3.

### Router codes as DG keys on a Qwen MoE

The hypothesis is that concatenated top-k router selections of a pretrained Qwen MoE are a *clustering* code. Near-duplicate contexts should then share more of their selected experts than their hidden-state cosine implies. As write keys they should then lose to a random sparse expansion of h_t when stored items are similar, and do comparably when items are distinct. The first stage is measurement only. On Qwen3-30B-A3B (128/8, no shared expert) and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (hybrid GDN; configuration provisional), record router top-k sets for paraphrase pairs, minimal-edit pairs and unrelated pairs. Plot Jaccard overlap of the expert sets against hidden-state cosine at the tap layer. The second stage uses as write keys for the existing side-channel memory (i) dense h_t, (ii) the learned DG keys of W4, (iii) FlyHash of h_t at matched active-bit count, and (iv) the multi-layer router code. The metrics are needle recall with 2–8 interfering pairs that share a template, interference between similar pairs, and the recency probe. This extends W4, T13 and notebook item R1, and it is the experiment that tests the DG-as-MoE premise directly.

### Expert neurogenesis for continual fine-tuning

The hypothesis is that adding newborn experts to a pretrained MoE and confining updates to them and to their router columns limits forgetting at least as well as tuning existing task-relevant experts (ESFT, Wang et al. 2024c). Conditioning the newborn experts' router bias on novelty should help further. The design uses Qwen3-30B-A3B. Add E_new experts per MoE layer, initialised by copying the most-routed expert plus noise or at zero output, with a router bias that decays with "age". Train on a sequence of new-fact and new-domain tasks. The baselines are full fine-tuning, LoRA, ESFT and the repository's replay plus sleep (M3). The metrics are new-task accuracy, forgetting on held-out general benchmarks and fact-recall sets, router load drift and newborn-expert recruitment rate. This extends `DentateGyrus.grow()` and M3, and it is the natural meeting point with the author's subnetwork-restricted fine-tuning project. The subnetwork becomes "experts that the new data selectively routes to", the same selectivity criterion as Lin et al. 2025. This run requires a GPU.

### Treves–Rolls with a completion stage

The hypothesis is that, once CA3 completion exists, a dense direct-path cue plus recurrent completion matches or beats a DG-coded read at high cue noise when storage used DG keys. The design adds one to three iterations of softmax Hopfield retrieval (the existing `ca3.py`) after the read in the temporal-loop ablation. Compare direct + completion, DG read + completion, and the current two arms over σ ∈ {0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3}, with at least three seeds. The metric is recall versus σ. This re-runs T16 under the conditions the hypothesis actually specifies.

### Detonator-gated write strength

The hypothesis is that making the write strength β_t depend on short-term "facilitation" of the active DG units speeds learning without the instability seen when DG and novelty learning rates were stacked (M2). Facilitation here means an exponential trace of each unit's recent activity. The design is a single new write-gain term in the temporal loop and GDN memory, compared against constant β, learned β, and CA1-novelty β with warm-up. The metrics are steps to 0.5 recall and newer-versus-older recall. This extends M2 and T13.

### Recruitment audit for newborn units

The hypothesis is that newborn units without an excitability bias are rarely recruited, so `grow()` has little effect. With a decaying bias they should preferentially encode novel inputs, the integration role proposed by Aimone et al. The design runs the continual-learning protocol of M3 with reserve units, logging per-unit win rates, code overlap for temporally adjacent items, and backward transfer, with and without the bias. This extends M3 and the neurogenesis path that is currently untested.

## Open questions

Whether any learned router, as opposed to a random or homeostatically constrained expansion, can be trained to separate rather than cluster is unresolved. The load-balancing objective of [Qiu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11873) pushes experts toward domain specialisation, which favours clustering. Expert-choice routing ([Zhou et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09368)) enforces lifetime sparseness, which is closer to the biology. A second open question is where a DG code should be computed in a hybrid model. The repository found that side-channel recall on Qwen3.5 depends on GDN layers downstream of the tap, so a DG key computed at one depth may not be readable by memories at another. A third is whether the separation signal belongs to granule cells or to mossy cells ([GoodSmith et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.12.026)). If mossy cells, the relevant architectural motif is an excitatory feedback loop within the DG, not a feed-forward expansion. A fourth is whether sparse, successor-bound codes support the path-following retrieval of [Samsonovich & Ascoli 2005](https://doi.org/10.1101/lm.85205) through chains of states in the GDN memory, rather than one-step recall. Finally, single seeds and 0.1–0.4M-parameter models cannot separate effects of a few points. Every claim in this track, including the T13 recency win, needs at least three seeds before it informs design on Qwen.
